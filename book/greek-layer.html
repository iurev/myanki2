Greek words did not come to Portuguese from Greece.
They came through Latin, and Latin respelled them.

Greek had sounds Latin did not. Roman writers wrote them with two letters — ph, th, ch — and used the Greek letter y for a vowel they did not own. Portuguese later simplified all of it. Those simplifications are still visible, and they are a reliable sign that a word passed through Greek.

Through Latin — Portuguese borrowed almost no word directly from Greek. A Greek word reached Roman writing first, was spelled the Latin way, and only then reached Portuguese. So the Greek is real, and the spelling you see is Latin's.

Three spellings that give a Greek word away

Latin needed two letters for three Greek sounds, and borrowed one Greek letter outright. Portuguese kept none of it. When you see f where English writes ph, or t where English writes th, you are looking at a Greek word that English respelled less than Portuguese did.

The payoff

Words ending in -ma are masculine

Portuguese nouns in -a are almost all feminine, so these look like an exception you must memorise. They are not. Greek had a third gender, neuter, and its neuter nouns ended in -ma. Latin kept them neuter. Portuguese lost the neuter and sent every one of them to the masculine, ending and all.

Topic levelA1— one rule, six words, and it stops a gender mistake you would otherwise make every time.

Neuter — a third gender, beside masculine and feminine. Latin and Greek had it; Portuguese does not. When Portuguese dropped it, neuter nouns had to go somewhere, and these went to the masculine.

The rule is worth stating in reverse too, because that is how you will use it: if a word ends in -ma and names something abstract or technical, say o, not a. The everyday feminine words in -ma — cama, forma, lama — are ordinary Latin words that never went near Greek.

About the level flags. A1 A2 B1 are CEFR levels. Your deck stores no level for nouns, adjectives or phrases. It does mark 145 verb cards (A1), but that is the level of the course book they were taken from, not a judgement about each word. Everything else on the page is a noun and carries no mark at all. So the flags here are an estimate, following how common the word is in everyday European Portuguese.

Where the origin is not firm. Two rows carry a dashed Sources disagree mark. papel reaches Greek πάπυρος through Latin papȳrus, but Greek itself very likely took the word from Egyptian, so the Greek step is real and the Greek origin is not. cadeira is agreed to come from cathedra ← καθέδρα; what is disputed is whether Portuguese inherited it through speech or took it from writing, and that decides which group it belongs to on this page.
