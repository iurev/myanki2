Greek words did not come to Portuguese from Greece.
They came through Latin, and Latin respelled them.

Greek had sounds Latin did not. Latin writers wrote them with two letters — ph, th, ch — and used the Greek letter y for a vowel they did not own. Portuguese later simplified all of it: ph to f, th to t, y to i, and ch to c. Those simplifications are still visible, and they are a good sign that a word passed through Greek.

Through Latin — Portuguese borrowed almost no word directly from Greek. A Greek word reached Latin writing first, was spelled the Latin way, and only then reached Portuguese. Some of those Latin spellings are two thousand years old and some were made by scholars in the Middle Ages, but the road is the same one. So the Greek is real, and the spelling you see is Latin's.

Main chart

Four spellings that give a Greek word away

Latin needed two letters for three Greek sounds, and borrowed one Greek letter outright. Portuguese kept none of it. When you see f where English writes ph, or t where English writes th, you are looking at a Greek word that English respelled less than Portuguese did.

The chart shows the three main ones. A fourth is rarer and gets its own small table below: Greek χ was written ch in Latin, and Portuguese writes it c — charta to carta.

Topic levelA1— covers all 15 cards that came with Latin, the 5 that came through speech, and the 11 words with no card beside them.

Fingerprint — a letter in the Portuguese word that only got there because the word passed through Greek and then through Latin spelling. There are four on this page: f for ph, t for th, c for ch, and i for y. A fingerprint is good evidence and never proof: plenty of Portuguese words have an f or a t for ordinary Latin reasons.

The payoff

Words ending in -ma are masculine

Portuguese nouns in -a are almost all feminine, so these look like a list you must memorise. There is a reason behind them. Greek had a third gender, neuter, and a large group of its neuter nouns ended in -μα. Latin kept them neuter. Portuguese lost the neuter and sent nearly all of them to the masculine, ending and all.

Topic levelA1— one rule, six words, one minimal pair, and it stops a gender mistake you would otherwise make every time.

Each card above is coloured by the road that word took, in the same colours the rest of the page uses, and the road is written out under the word as well. These six took two of the three roads, and the rule holds on both: problema came with Latin, clima is a modern arrival, and both are masculine.

Neuter — a third gender, beside masculine and feminine. Latin and Greek had it; Portuguese does not. When Portuguese dropped it, neuter nouns had to go somewhere, and these went to the masculine.

The rule is worth stating in reverse too, because that is how you will use it: if a word ends in -ma and names something abstract or technical, say o, not a. The everyday feminine words in -ma — cama, forma, lama — are plain Latin words. Priberam gives late Latin cama, Latin forma and Latin lama, and none of them comes from a Greek neuter in -μα.

The rule is not watertight, so here is the leak. a asma, asthma, does come from a Greek neuter — ἆσθμα (âsthma) — and Portuguese made it feminine anyway. It is the one common exception, and it is a medical word. Every abstract or technical -ma noun a learner meets is masculine.

The colours mean something different on each layer. Here the colour is how the Greek word reached Portuguese: blue = came with Latin, green = a modern arrival, orange = came through ordinary speech, purple = not Greek at all. Colour is never the only channel; every group is named in its table header, in the legend, and — in the -ma grid — in a line under each word. matemática is printed with the contrast cards but is coloured blue, because it is Greek and the purple would say otherwise. The -ma grid mixes blue and green on purpose: cinema, programa and clima are modern arrivals and problema, sistema and tema came with Latin. Two places use blue for something else and say so here: the big arrow at the top and the bottom row of the fingerprint chart, where the blue marks the spelling change itself and not a group of words.

The Greek script. Every Greek word on the page is printed next to its Latin spelling or its transliteration, and its meaning is given in English, so nothing on the page depends on being able to read Greek. Greek and Latin forms carry no level flag: they are evidence, not vocabulary.

About the level flags. A1 A2 B1 are CEFR levels. Your deck stores no level for nouns, adjectives or phrases. It does mark 145 verb cards (A1), but that is the level of the course book they were taken from, not a judgement about each word. One verb card appears here, telefonar a, and it carries that mark; this page flags it A1 as well, so no flag here sits above the deck. Everything else on the page is a noun and carries no mark at all. So the flags here are an estimate, following how common the word is in everyday European Portuguese.
