Latin had no lh and no nh.
Portuguese built both, out of letters Latin already had.

Forty-four cards in your deck carry one of these two letter pairs. That is more cards than any other layer in this book. Four different histories put them there: three sound changes, each leaving its own clue in the Latin word, and one set of words that arrived from another language.

Fifteen more words belong on this page and are not in your deck. They are marked no card and they are all A1 to B1. Six of them are A1, so they are worth adding today.

Yod — the sound at the start of English yes. In Latin, an i standing before another vowel was said as a yod: fīlium was said fee-lyum. That yod is the main builder on this page.

Palatal — a sound made with the middle of the tongue against the roof of the mouth. That part of the mouth is called the palate. lh and nh are the two Portuguese palatals. Spanish writes them ll and ñ. Italian writes them gli and gn.

Sound law — a change in the way words are said. It hit every word that had that sound in that place. If you know the law, you can work out words you have never seen.

A star, and an apostrophe — a star means nobody ever wrote that Latin word down. Scholars work it back from the modern words. *maneāna is a good guess, not a record. A word with no star is written down somewhere. An apostrophe, as in oc'lum, marks a vowel that dropped out when people said the word.

Evidence · the letter l

What happened to a Latin l depended on what stood next to it

olho and óculos are the same Latin word. It came into Portuguese twice, hundreds of years apart, and you now learn the two results as two separate cards. The table below holds four of your cards. One letter, four different results. The last two rows are that pair.

Topic levelA1— 1 of the 44 cards, olho, and 3 contrast cards (2 A1, 1 A2). Small, and it settles the rule.

The same three letters, cul, in the same Latin word. People said the first word every day, and it wore down to lh. Scholars copied the second one out of a book much later, so it kept every letter. Layer 01 showed the same split with ch· and pl·.
Evidence · the letter n

Layer 02 said the n disappears. After an i it came back.

On layer 02 you saw manum lose its n and become mão. The same letter in the same position gives vinho, with the n still standing. The vowel in front decides.

Topic levelA1— 1 of the 44 cards, vinho branco, and 3 contrast cards (2 A1, 1 A2). Read this even if you skip the rest.

after amanum→mãohand · card 0260 · A1
after ivīnum→vinhowine · card 0365 · A1
Both Latin words end the same way: vowel, n, um. Only the vowel in front is different, and only one of the two words still has an n.

Why it came back — the n did not survive as a consonant. It first became part of the vowel. That made the vowel nasal, exactly as in mão. A nasal vowel is one you say through the nose. But a nasal ĩ before another vowel is hard to say. So the nasal sound became a consonant again, this time on the palate: vīnum → vĩo → vinho. That is why the new letters are nh and not a plain n.

Back to layer 01

Latin cl became ch where a word or a piece of one began, and lh where a dropped vowel made it

Layer 01 was about words starting with cl-, pl-, fl-. This page is about a c and an l that were not together in Latin at all: a vowel stood between them, and only when that vowel fell did the two letters meet. That is the real difference — not front against middle. It is why clāvem gives chave while oculum, by way of oc'lum, gives olho. Layer 01’s own achar and encher have the cluster in the middle of the word and still give ch, because there it begins the piece after a prefix. Both changes are complete. A plain cl only survives in words that scholars brought back from Latin later.

Grammar you use every day

Why ter, vir and pôr grow an nh in the eu form

Layer 03 showed these three verbs with short, worn-down infinitives. Their eu form looks irregular. For two of them the yod explains it. The Latin ending already had a yod in it, so the same rule that built filho built tenho.

Topic levelA1— 3 verb cards, in none of the 44: ter, vir and pôr are all A1. You say these forms every day.

About the level flags. A1 A2 B1 B2 C1 are CEFR levels. Your deck stores no level for nouns, adjectives or phrases. It does mark 145 verb cards (A1), but that is the level of the course book they were taken from, not a judgement about each word — it covers mergulhar “to dive” as readily as falar. So the flags here are still an estimate, and four of them sit above that mark on purpose: sonhar and apanhar are flagged A2, mergulhar and reconhecer B1, and all four cards carry the (A1) mark. pôr is flagged A1 here and on layer 03. They follow how common the word is in everyday European Portuguese and where it normally appears in A1–B1 course books. Treat them as a reading order, not as a rule. If a word feels easy and it says B1, learn it.

Where the origin is not firm. Eleven rows are marked Starred form: escolher, trabalhar, mergulhar, se calhar, amanhã, de manhã, até amanhã, manhã, olhar para, olhar por and olhe. Their Latin source was never written down. Scholars work a form like that back from the modern words and put a star in front of it. It is a good guess, not a record. Four rows are marked Sources disagree, and each prints both readings. apanhar is a borrowing from Spanish apañar in the main dictionaries and a starred Latin verb in older books; the page follows the dictionaries and puts it in the borrowed group. ilha is either a Catalan borrowing or Portuguese work on īnsulam. brilhante is a borrowing either way, but sources split on whether the lh arrived with it or was put in here. For desenhar, the Portuguese dictionaries go straight to Latin dēsignāre and Wiktionary goes through Italian disegnare. On the second reading it would sit in the borrowed group, not with the yod. The last mark is Analogy, on the pôr table. Latin pōnō has no yod and should have given *pono. Portuguese copied the shape from tenho and venho instead. Copying one word's shape onto another is called analogy. That table is therefore grey, not purple: ponho is not a borrowing, and no builder on this page made it.

No dates for the borrowings. The page says which language each of the six borrowed words came from, but not which century. To give a century you need a dated corpus, and this book does not have one. A date you cannot check is not evidence.
