Latin had one ending for “the thing to do with X”.
Portuguese turned it into -eiro, and still uses it every day.

The other layers are changes that finished long ago. This one has not finished. You can put -eiro on a noun today and a Portuguese speaker will understand the word you just made. So it is worth knowing which of your cards were built with it, and which only look as if they were.

Nineteen cards carry the letters -eir-: eighteen nouns that end in -eiro or -eira, and one verb. Only ten of the nineteen come from the Latin ending. Eleven more words belong here and are not in your deck, marked no card.

Suffix — a piece added to the end of a word that changes what it means. -eiro is a suffix. So is the English -er in baker, and it does the same job.

Productive — an ending is productive when speakers still use it to build new words. -eiro is productive. A new job or a new gadget can take it tomorrow.

Sources disagree — a row with a dashed edge and this mark. The dictionaries do not agree about where the word came from. Both readings are printed in the row, and neither is hidden.

The look-alikes

The five weekday cards are not built with this ending

Portuguese is the only major Romance language that counts its weekdays. French, Spanish and Italian all use the planets and the old gods, and so does English. Portuguese numbers them instead, and the word it numbers is feira — Latin fēria, a day of the Church's calendar. Nothing was added to that word. The letters at the end of segunda-feira are part of feira itself, not an ending put on it.

Topic levelA1— 5 cards, all A1, plus the 2 contrast cards, also A1, which are in no total. You use these every week, so the story is worth two minutes.

an endingiānuārium→janeiroJanuary · card 0634 · A1
a whole wordfēria→feiraa day of the Church year · inside cards 0627–0631 · A1
The coloured part of each Latin word is the part that gave the letters at the end. In janeiro it is an ending, put on the name Janus. In segunda-feira it is a whole word. On its own today feira means a market. The fairs were held on the Church's feast days, so the word moved with them. Inside the weekday names it still carries the older sense, a day. Both words end in ·eir· and a vowel, and the two are built in opposite ways.

na or à — both put a day into a sentence, and they do not mean the same thing. Na quarta-feira vou ao médico means one Wednesday, the one you are talking about. À quarta-feira vou ao médico means every Wednesday. The plural says the same: às quartas-feiras. sábado and domingo are masculine, so they take no and ao instead: no sábado for one Saturday, ao sábado for every Saturday.

Still working

You can build these yourself

This ending has not stopped. Take a noun you already have on a card, add -eiro or -eira, and you get the person who handles it, the thing that holds it, or the tree it grows on. Cut the last vowel of the noun first: cozinha gives cozinh-, laranja gives laranj-. A noun ending in -m writes it as -n-: jardim gives jardin-. Every result below is a real word. Three of the five have no card.

Topic levelA2— 5 base words, all cards you already have. The results are 4 A2 and 1 B1: two are cards, three have no card.

A warning — the ending is regular but the meaning is not always guessable. banheiro should mean “the person who bathes”. In Portugal it means a lifeguard, and in Brazil it means the bathroom itself. Build words to read them, not to say them.

And it does not always fit — pão plus -eiro does not give padeiro. That word was built on pada, an old word for a loaf, so the baker is not in the table above. When the base has changed shape, the sum stops working.

The marked letter in each Latin word is the one an older layer worked on. In dinheiro the n fell out and left the vowel nasal, and the nasal later hardened back into nh — the same road as vinho, which is layer 04. Old Portuguese wrote the word dĩeiro in between. In candeeiro the l is struck through because it went away: an l between two vowels disappeared, which is layer 02. That l sits in the base word, not in the ending, so layer 02 did not need the ending at all. Losing it left the two e's of candeeiro side by side. Nothing is coloured on the Portuguese side of that row, because what layer 02 did was take a letter out.

Deck check

Six things this layer found in your own cards

Topic levelA1— 6 findings: three about A1 words, two about A2 words, one about a B1 word.

primeiro and maneira have no cards

Both are A1 and both are built with this ending. primeiro is the ordinary word for first. You already say the next two numbers every week: segunda is second and terça is third. maneira is the everyday word for way or manner, and it turns up in de qualquer maneira and desta maneira.

Suggested cards: primeiro (primeira, -s): first, maneira (f, -s): way, manner

Your job words stop at five

The deck has cozinheiro, enfermeiro, engenheiro, faroleiro and prisioneiro, but not bombeiro, padeiro, jardineiro or barbeiro — all A2, all more common in ordinary life than a lighthouse keeper.

Suggested cards: bombeiro (m, -s): firefighter, padeiro (m, -s): baker, jardineiro (m, -s): gardener, barbeiro (m, -s): barber

Five cards teach feira and none defines it A1

Cards 0627 to 0631 all contain feira, and the word has no card. It means a market or a fair, it is used on its own constantly, and knowing it is what makes the weekday names stop being five random shapes. On its own it works like this: Vou à feira ao sábado. — I go to the market on Saturdays.

Suggested card: feira (f, -s): market, fair

Nine cards carry no example sentence A1

Cards 0627 to 0635 are bare phrase cards: the front holds the English, and the back holds only the Portuguese word again. Seven of them are on this page as cards, and two more, sábado and domingo, are here for contrast. All nine had to be given a sentence written for this book. They are the nine rows tagged new.

Suggested edit: give each back the two lines the noun cards have — the Portuguese sentence, then the English. For card 0627: À segunda-feira trabalho em casa. / On Mondays I work at home.

Card 0439 has an English error

The back of engenheiro reads He is a engineer. It needs an before a vowel sound: He is an engineer.

Suggested edit: He is an engineer.

Card 0349 says I like pan

The back of frigideira reads Eu gosto de frigideira. / I like pan. The English is not a sentence anybody would say, and pan is not the right word for a frigideira, which is a frying pan. The Portuguese is also odd: you like the food, not the pan.

Suggested edit: Eu uso a frigideira. / I use the frying pan.

About the level flags. A1 A2 B1 B2 C1 are CEFR levels. Your deck stores no level for nouns, adjectives or phrases. It does mark 145 verb cards (A1), but that is the level of the course book they were taken from, not a judgement about each word — it covers mergulhar “to dive” as readily as falar. One card on this page carries that mark: cheirar a, card 0040. This page flags it A2, one step above the deck, and the row says so. So the flags here are still an estimate. They follow how common the word is in everyday European Portuguese and where it normally appears in A1–B1 course books. Treat them as a reading order, not as a rule. If a word feels easy and it says B1, learn it.

Where the origin is not firm. Three rows carry a dashed edge and the mark sources disagree, and each one prints both readings. frigideira: the dictionaries split it as frigir plus one suffix, -deira. On that split the word does not belong on this page. Split -deira again — it was built from Latin -tu plus -āria — and it does. cheirar a: Latin had two verbs, flagrāre “to burn” and frāgrāre “to smell sweet”, already confused with each other by Roman times, and the dictionaries file the word under one or the other. The form, though, is settled: fr· stays fr· in Portuguese — your own frio and frigideira show it — so only fl· can have given this ch·. What is disputed is the meaning and the dictionary entry, not the sound. Layer 01 carries the same card, the same mark and the same reasoning. prisioneiro: Priberam builds it on prisão. Infopédia and Wiktionary take it from French prisonnier.

The three doubts do not weigh the same. cheirar a is a look-alike under either verb, so that doubt changes nothing here. prisioneiro stays with the people under either reading, because French -ier and Portuguese -eiro go back to the same Latin -ārium; only the road changes. The frigideira doubt is the one that changes something, and it is the reason that word sits with the look-alikes instead of with the words the ending built. A grey row background is not a mark: it only says the row carries a note underneath. No form on this page is worked back from nothing, so no row carries the other dashed mark, starred form. That one is used on layers 05 and 06, for a source form nobody ever wrote down.
