Portuguese has two verbs for “to be”.
One of them used to mean “to sit”. The other used to mean “to stand”.

Latin had one verb for “to be”: esse. Portuguese kept its present and its imperfect. The infinitive, the future and the past were all lost. An ordinary verb stepped into the gap: sedēre, “to sit”. A second ordinary verb, stāre, “to stand”, became a second word for “to be” — estar. The past came from somewhere else again. So ser and ir each hold three old Latin verbs, joined into one. Dictionaries do not fully agree about the infinitive ser itself; the chart below prints both answers.

Suppletion — the word for this. It means one verb is built from parts of several different older verbs. It is why ser and ir look irregular. They are not irregular. They are mixed.

The star * — a form with a star was never written down. Nobody found it in a Latin text. Experts worked it back from the modern words. *h₁es- is a root of Proto-Indo-European, the shared ancestor of Latin, English, German and Russian. *essere, *stēvī and *sedentāre are later Latin forms nobody wrote down. Forms with no star are real Latin, printed in books we still have.

Who is on this page, and why. Three reasons, in this order.
ser A1to be Mixed. Built from three Latin verbs: esse, sedēre and fuī.
ir A1to go Mixed. Built from three Latin verbs: īre, vādere and fuī. Its past tense is the same as the past tense of ser — both use fui, foste, foi. That shared past is why the two verbs are on one page.
estar A1to be (right now) Support, not mixed. One Latin verb, stāre, in every tense but the past. It is here because you cannot use ser correctly without it.
pôr · ter · vir · ver A1put · have · come · see Contrast. These four are not mixed, and they are on the page to show what a short infinitive looks like when suppletion is not the reason. Their story is a lost letter, not a lost verb.

The parts

The six Latin verbs on this page

Five of them went into ser and ir. The sixth, stāre, gave estar — every tense but the past. Each one has its own colour, and the colours are used again in every table on this page. Every row also names its Latin verb in words, so the colour is never the only clue.

One of the six is not a verb you could look up in a Latin dictionary. fuī is the past tense of esse, but it comes from a different root, *bʰuH-. Latin esse was already mixed. It is counted on its own here because that is what it acts like.

Topic levelA2— background. Read it once. You do not have to remember the Latin, only that there is more than one verb inside.

Two different verbs. One past tense.
ser to befui · foste · foi · fomos · foram
ir to gofui · foste · foi · fomos · foram
the same five words

Why. Latin esse already had a strange past: fuī, which belongs to a different root. Latin īre had its own past, iī / īvī, and lost it. Portuguese then gave ir the past of ser. So the sharing happened twice: once inside Latin, once inside Portuguese.

The Latin, printed. fuī · fuistī · fuit · fuimus · fuērunt → fui · foste · foi · fomos · foram. Five forms in, five forms out, and both Portuguese verbs use all five.

English does this too. “I have been to Lisbon” means “I went to Lisbon”. English uses a form of be for going somewhere, exactly where Portuguese uses fui. English went is borrowed too: it was the past of wend, a different verb.

Eu fui estudante. I was a student. — this is sernew
Eu fui à escola. I went to school. — this is irnew

How to tell them apart. Look at what comes after the verb. A place (à escola, ao Porto, à praia) → it is ir, “went”. A job, a name or a thing (estudante, professor, biólogo) → it is ser, “was”.

The future is two words stuck together
ser to beser + ei · ás · á · emos · ão → serei · serás · será · seremos · serão
ir to goir + ei · ás · á · emos · ão → irei · irás · irá · iremos · irão
those endings are the short present of haver: hei · hás · há · hemos · hão

Why. Latin had a future tense of its own — erō “I will be”, ībō “I will go”. Portuguese lost both. It built a new future out of the infinitive plus the present of haver “to have”. So serei is ser + hei, and irei is ir + hei. Spanish, French and Italian all built their future the same way.

Why hemos and not havemos. The nós ending is -emos, not -avemos. The short form hemos is what got stuck on the infinitive. On its own today haver says havemos, so the ending and the living verb no longer match.

What that means for the colours. serei and irei are coloured by their infinitive, because that is the half that carries the verb. The ending is haver in every verb in the language, so it is not part of what makes ser and ir mixed.

Não há pão. There is no bread. — há is the same verb, still alive on its ownno card
Um dia hei de ver o mar. One day I will see the sea. — the old two-word form, still usedno card

Missing card. haver A1 is not in your deck, in any form. há “there is / there are” is one of the first hundred words of the language and it is the strongest candidate for a new card on this page.

Topic levelA2— nothing to learn here. Read it once and the mixing stops looking odd.

é and is go back to one root. fui and been go back to another. English mixes its verbs in the same way, so this is not something strange about Portuguese.

Practical

Which one to use

The old meanings still work. A thing that sits stays the same. A thing that stands can move or change.

A different reason

Short infinitive, full stem

These four verbs are not mixed. They have a different problem: a consonant between two vowels dropped out. It dropped out of the infinitive but stayed in the other forms, so the infinitive is the odd one, not the stem. Learn the Latin word and the forms stop being a surprise.

Three of them lost an n, which is layer 02. ver lost a d instead — a different letter and a rarer loss, but the same kind of hole in the same place. The lost letter is marked like this in each Latin word, so you can see it even in black and white.

Topic levelA1— ter, vir, ver and pôr are all A1. Learn the four stems, not the four infinitives.

pôr is the only verb in Portuguese that ends in neither -ar, -er nor -ir. Same change, taken one step further: pōnere → põer → pôr. Losing the n pulled the two vowels together, and the ending went with them.

What the tags mean. A plain tag (card 0159, in deck) means the sentence is a deck card's own text, copied exactly. A dashed new means the sentence was written for this page. A dotted no card on a word means your deck does not contain that word at all. There are 12 of those: presente, ausente, interesse, futuro, sentar-se, presidente, sessão, custar, estado, distância, instante in the family tables, and haver in the future-tense box. They are counted on their own and never added into the verb counts or the 14 deck words. The strongest candidates for new cards are haver — its present form há “there is” is one of the first hundred words of the language — sentar-se, which is the plain everyday verb for the meaning this whole page is built on, and presente, which is A1 in both its meanings, “present” and “gift”.

Reconstructed forms. A star means the form was never written down and was worked back from later languages: *h₁es-, *bʰuH-, *sed-, *h₁ey-, *weh₂dʰ- and *steh₂- are Proto-Indo-European roots; *essere, *stēvī and *sedentāre are unwritten later Latin. Everything without a star is attested Latin. Where two sources give different parents for the same word, the mark is a dashed sources disagree outline, both readings are printed, and a line says which part is argued about. It appears on the hero and on exactly two rows: the infinitive ser, where the form is settled and the parent verb is not, and the past estive, where the forms are settled and the source of the stem is not. The ser dispute passes down to serei and sido, which are built on the infinitive; the notes say so. A grey label with no outline, such as vamos: see below, is only a pointer to a note and is not a dispute.

About the level flags. A1 A2 B1 B2 C1 are CEFR levels. Your deck stores no level for nouns, adjectives or phrases. It does mark 145 verb cards (A1), but that is the level of the course book they were taken from, not a judgement about each word — it covers mergulhar “to dive” as readily as falar. So the flags here are still an estimate, and a few of them sit above that mark on purpose. For words they follow how common the word is in everyday European Portuguese. For tenses they follow the normal order of an A1–B1 course: present first, then imperfeito and pretérito, then futuro. Treat them as a reading order, not as a rule.

The colours. They mean something different on each layer. Here each colour is one Latin verb, and only that verb: ■ esse, ■ fuī, ■ sedēre, ■ īre, ■ vādere, ■ stāre. Nothing else on the page is coloured — the four short-infinitive verbs (pôr, ter, vir, ver) come from none of the six, so they are printed in plain ink with the lost letter double-underlined instead. One table inside a coloured verb is plain ink too: the past of estar, because the parent of estive is disputed and a colour there would settle an argument the page says is open. Every coloured row also names its Latin verb in words, so the page works in black and white. One exception, on purpose: in the family tables at the end the colour marks the root, not the single verb, because that is what those tables are grouped by. Any word that reached Portuguese by a different verb — cadeira, existir, assistir a, restaurante — sits in its own labelled block with its real source printed beside it.

The tables are European Portuguese. The second person plural (sois, ides, estais) is left out of every table because EP does not use it. It appears only in running text, where the history needs it: the old ides is one of the last traces of īre in the present tense.
