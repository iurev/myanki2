Portuguese has two verbs for “to be”.
One of them used to mean “to sit”. The other used to mean “to stand”.

Latin had one verb for “to be”: esse. Most of its forms did not survive. Portuguese replaced them with two normal verbs: sedēre (to sit) and stāre (to stand). The tenses that were still missing were filled with parts of other verbs. So ser and ir are not one verb each. Each one is three or four old Latin verbs joined together.

Suppletion — the word for this. It means one verb is built from parts of several different older verbs. It is why ser and ir look irregular. They are not irregular. They are mixed.

This page covers three verbs. They are here for two different reasons.
ser A1to be Mixed. Built from two Latin verbs: esse and sedēre.
ir A1to go Mixed. Built from three Latin verbs. Its past tense is the same as the past tense of ser — both use fui, foste, foi. That shared past is why the two verbs are on one page.
estar A1to be (right now) Not mixed. One clean Latin verb, stāre. It is here because you cannot use ser correctly without it.

Where every tense comes from

Only the two mixed verbs, ser and ir. One line per tense. The colour is the Latin verb that tense came from. When the colour changes, you are looking at a different verb.

Two different verbs. One past tense.
ser to befui · foste · foi · fomos · foram
ir to gofui · foste · foi · fomos · foram
the same five words

Why. Latin sedēre (sit) and īre (go) both lost their own past tense. Both verbs then took the past of a third Latin verb, fuī. So now they share it.

English does this too. “I have been to Lisbon” means “I went to Lisbon”. English also uses the verb be to talk about going somewhere. Portuguese does the same thing, in the past tense.

Eu fui estudante. I was a student. — this is sernew
Eu fui à escola. I went to school. — this is irnew

How to tell them apart. Look at what comes after the verb. A place (à escola, ao Porto, à praia) → it is ir, “went”. A job, a thing or a description (estudante, professor, feliz) → it is ser, “was”.

Compare

English does the same thing

“am / is / are”, “be / been” and “was / were” are three different old verbs with one name. Two of those verbs are the same two that Portuguese used.

é and is are the same word. fui and been are the same word. English mixes its verbs in the same way, so this is not something strange about Portuguese.

A different reason

Short infinitive, full stem

These four verbs are not mixed. They have a different problem, and it is the change from layer 02: a consonant between two vowels disappeared. It disappeared in the infinitive but stayed in all the other forms. So the infinitive is the odd one, not the stem. Learn the Latin word and the forms stop being a surprise.

Topic levelA1— ter, vir, ver and pôr are all A1.

pôr is the only verb in Portuguese that does not end in -ar, -er or -ir. Same change. In pôr it removed the ending itself.

Practical

Which one to use

The old meanings still work. A thing that sits stays the same. A thing that stands can move or change.

ser

sit — what a thing is

  • job, role: Eu sou estudante.
  • name: Ele é o João.
  • type or material: A mesa é de madeira.
  • where you are from: Eu sou de Lisboa.
  • telling the time: São duas horas.
estar

stand — where a thing is now

  • place: O gato está atrás da árvore.
  • feeling: Eu estou cansado.
  • weather now: Está frio.
  • something happening now: Ele está a comer.
  • anything that can change today: A porta está aberta.
