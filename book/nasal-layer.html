Origin layer 02 · European Portuguese · 18 noun cards end in ·ão or ·ã · 25 words with no card
·n· ·l· Between two vowels, Latin n and l stopped being spoken. Portuguese never put them back.

Portuguese dropped two letters from the middle of Latin words.
The plural still shows which Latin ending was there.

Latin had three different endings: -anem, -anum and -ōnem. After the n was dropped, all three became one ending in Portuguese: -ão. So the singular tells you nothing. The plural still tells you everything.

Between two vowels — the rule only worked when the letter had a vowel on each side. canem lost its n, and sōlum lost its l, because both letters had a vowel in front and a vowel behind. sōlem “sun” kept its l: Portuguese dropped the ending -em very early, so the l came to stand last in the word with nothing behind it.

Nasal vowel — the sound written ão, ã, õe. The n is gone, but you still send the air through the nose. This is what makes Portuguese sound different from Spanish.

The mechanism

What each dropped letter left behind

When l was dropped, the two vowels simply met. When n was dropped, the vowels met and stayed nasal. That nasal sound is all that is left of the n. At the end of a word the nasal sound stayed. In the middle of a word it later faded away.

Topic levelA1— 16 of the 18 cards in the six tables below are A1, the other two are A2. Fifteen more words fit here and have no card, lua and dor first among them. This is the part to read today.

Old pt. — Portuguese as it was written before about 1500. Some rows print it, because it is the step between the Latin word and the modern one. bonum → old pt. bõo → bom. Old Portuguese is evidence, not vocabulary: those forms carry no level flag and you are never meant to learn them.

Main chart

Three Latin endings, one Portuguese ending, three plurals

Latin had three endings that all ended up as -ão. The plural did not merge. So the plural is not random: it is the old Latin ending, still working.

Topic levelA2— you need this the day you start using plurals. 12 of the 18 cards are A1 or A2, and 11 more words with no card are sorted into the same groups below.

Latin -anem, or any word that reached Portuguese as -an → plural -ães Latin -anum, feminine -anam → plural -ãos, -ãs Latin -ōnem, and the -ão ending grown from it → plural -ões does not follow its Latin ending

Read the four lines again. In the singular, canem and manum arrive at the same word ending. In the plural, canēs and manūs do not. Portuguese kept the Latin difference in the plural and lost it in the singular.

What to do with this. You cannot see the Latin ending, so learn the plural together with the word, the way you already learn the gender. Three plural groups cover sixteen of your eighteen cards, and one group alone holds ten of them.

One ending you can guess

English -tion is Portuguese -ção

Latin -tiōnem became -tion in English and -ção in Portuguese. Gender and plural come free: always feminine, always -ções. Every one of them belongs to the ·ões group above, because -tiōnem is -ōnem with a t in front.

Topic levelA2— the rule is A2. 5 cards and 4 words with no card. Single words in it can be B1 or B2, and you do not need to learn them one by one.

The grey rows are the four words your deck does not have. They were built with the rule, not looked up: take the English word, cut -tion, write -ção. That is the point of the group — one rule replaces a word list. Those four are counted on their own, apart from the 25 words with no card in the tables above. The colours mean something different on each layer. Here the colour is the Latin ending the word’s plural comes from: blue = -anem, green = -anum and its feminine -anam, orange = -ōnem, purple = does not follow its ending. A few words are painted for the ending they behave like, not the ending they descend from, and each of those rows says so: alemão and capitão are blue because they reached Portuguese as ·an, avião, portão and televisão are orange because their ·ão is the later suffix that grew out of -ōnem, and limão is orange because it arrived from Arabic ending in ·on and joined the group. About the level flags. A1 A2 B1 B2 C1 are CEFR levels. Your deck stores no level for nouns, adjectives or phrases. It does mark 145 verb cards (A1), but that is the level of the course book they were taken from, not a judgement about each word — it covers mergulhar “to dive” as readily as falar. Three verbs on this page carry that mark — sair, ter and pôr — and this page flags all three A1 as well, so no flag here sits above the deck. A fourth verb, vir, carries a chapter marker instead, (ch.4), which is not a level. pôr is flagged A1 on layers 03 and 04 too. Everything else on the page is a noun, an adjective, a number or a phrase, and carries no mark at all. So the flags here are an estimate. They follow how common the word is in everyday European Portuguese and where it normally appears in A1–B1 course books. Treat them as a reading order, not as a rule. If a word feels easy and it says B1, learn it.

Where the origin is not firm. Three rows carry a dashed Sources disagree mark and print every reading. coração: Wiktionary builds it on a Vulgar Latin *corāceōnem, Priberam on a Vulgar Latin *corātiōnem, and Corominas, writing on Spanish corazón, on plain cor with a big-thing ending. All three end in ·ōnem, so the plural is not in doubt. capitão: Wiktionary and Ciberdúvidas send it through Genoese capitan from Late Latin capitānus; other dictionaries take it straight from Late Latin capitāneum. Both readings end at caput “head”. limão: the word is Arabic laymūn, from Persian, and sources split on whether Portuguese took it from Arabic directly or through Old French limon. Neither road is Latin. Its ·ões plural comes from the shape the word had when it arrived, limon. A grey row background is not a mark: it only says the row carries a note underneath.
