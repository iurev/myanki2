Latin had ct, pt and lt in the middle of words.
In Portuguese the first letter melted into a vowel.

The first consonant softened until it was no longer a consonant. It stayed in the word as part of the vowel in front of it. In noctem the c turned into the i of noite.

Eighteen cards belong here: seventeen the rule changed, and one that only looks changed. Ten more words belong here and are not in your deck, marked no card. Two of those ten are noite and muito, which you read constantly inside other cards and cannot study on their own.

Sound law — a change in the way words are said. It hit every word that had that sound in that place. If you know the law, you can work out words you have never seen.

Melted — the word this page uses for the change. The letter turned into a vowel sound and joined the vowel beside it, instead of dropping out of the word.

A star before a word means nobody ever wrote that word down. Scholars work it back from the modern words. A starred form is a good guess, not a record.

What is on this page

Eighteen cards, and why each one is here

Three groups were made by the rule and ended in different letters. One card only looks as if it belongs. Ten more words fit the page and have no card. Four cards are printed for contrast and are counted apart.

One rule, three results, one look-alike, ten missing words, and a contrast group.
Became ·it·15 cards The normal result. Latin ct, pt or lt in the middle of a word: the first letter became an i. noctem → noite, multās → muitas, acceptāre → aceitar.
Became ·ut· instead1 card outubro, and off the deck outro. The two got their u in different ways. In outubro a c melted, and the new sound settled on ou, while in oito the same c settled on oi. In outro it is the l that turned into a u, which is what an l before a consonant normally did.
Left no letter1 card lutar, and off the deck luta and escrito. The letter melted into an i as usual, and that i is no longer written. In escrito it fell together with an i that was already there. In luta it was dropped later, after centuries of luita. Nothing extra is left in the spelling, so the word looks untouched.
Only looks the same1 card fato “suit”. Latin factum did go through the rule, and it gave feito. The word for a suit is a different word — Gothic or Arabic, sources disagree — and it never had a ct at all.
Not in your deck10 words Common words that fit this page and have no card: noite, muito, outro, leite, direito, feito, peito, escrito, luta, estreito. All A1 to B1. Each is marked no card.
Here for contrast4 cards exato, teto, diretor and restrito all had ct in Latin and none of them melted, because all four were taken from written Latin centuries after the rule had stopped working. They show where the rule ends. They are not part of the 18.

Evidence

Two cards from the same Latin number

You learn oito as a number and outubro as a month. The same Latin number octō is inside both. October was the eighth month of the Roman year, which began in March. The number is still inside the name.

Topic levelA1— 2 cards from this layer, both A1, plus one old spelling that is only evidence. The table below adds 6 more cards you already have, also A1, that are not part of this layer. The best thing on the page to remember.

the numberoctō→oitoeight · card 0597 · A1
the monthoctōbrius→outubroOctober · card 0643 · A1
the month, thenoctōbrius→oitubrothe old spelling of the month · not a modern word, do not learn it
Both words begin oct- and both melted the c. The number settled on oi; the month settled on ou. Old Portuguese wrote the month both ways, oitubro and outubro, before outubro won. Other words still swap the two today — ouro and oiro for gold, touro and toiro for bull — though there the ou came out of a Latin au, not out of a ct.
The look-alike

fato did not come from factum

Card 0321 is fato, a suit. It has the shape of a word this rule made. It is not one. Latin factum went through the rule normally and came out as feito, which your deck does not have.

Topic levelA2— 2 words in the box below: 1 card and 1 missing word. Read this if you are ever tempted to guess an origin from spelling.

latinfactum→feitodone · no card · A2
not from latin*fat→fatosuit · card 0321 · A2
Two different words that ended up looking alike. Sources disagree about where fato itself came from: many say a Gothic word for clothing, Portuguese dictionaries say an Arabic one. They agree it is not factum. Portuguese then took factum a second time, straight from books. That one is spelled facto, “fact”, and in Portugal its c is still written and still said. So one Latin word gave two Portuguese words, feito and facto, and fato is a third word that only looks like them. Your deck has neither feito nor facto. feito is one of the ten missing words above; facto is a book word, like the four in the contrast section below, and this page does not count it.
The quiet case

Where the rule leaves no letter

The melted letter became an i. In these words that i is not written today. In one it fell together with an i that was already there. In the other it was dropped centuries later. The word then looks as if the rule skipped it.

Topic levelB1— the group is 3 words: 1 card and 2 missing words. Two of the three are shown below, with noite beside them for comparison. Small, and it explains two words that otherwise look broken.

latin, and the i showsnoctem→noitenight · no card · A1
latin → the 1200slucta→luitahow Portuguese wrote “fight” then · not a modern word
the 1200s → todayluita→lutafight · no card · B1
latin → todayscrīptum→escritowritten · no card · A2
The luita row is the proof that the rule did run in luta. Portuguese wrote that i for centuries, as luita and luitar, and dropped it only later — the same way fruito became fruto and truita became truta. Why the i went there and stayed in muito is not settled. Galician writes loita to this day. escrito shows no such step. Latin scrīptum already carried a long ī in that exact place, so a melted p would have had nothing to add. Nobody ever wrote an extra i there. escrito is in this group for its result, not for any written proof.
Here for contrast

Four cards where the ct never melted

The rule only worked on words that were handed down by speech. A word taken later out of written Latin arrived after the rule had stopped, so its ct was never melted. Your deck holds a whole set of these. Four are printed here, and two of the four have a spoken twin still in use: restrito beside estreito, and diretor beside direito.

Topic levelA2— 4 cards: 3 A2, 1 B1, plus the missing word estreito (B1) in the box below. Read it for the pattern. It tells you when not to expect an i.

by speechstrictum→estreitonarrow · no card · B1
from booksrestrictum→restritorestricted · card 0544 · B1
One Latin stem, strict-, two Portuguese words. The inherited one melted the c and grew an ei. The borrowed one melted nothing: its c was never said in Portuguese, and is not written. That gives you a test, and it works in one direction only. A word with ei or oi where Latin had ct came down by speech. A plain vowel means either a book word, like the four in the table above, or one of the three words on this page where the i is no longer written.

Deck check

Five things this layer found in your own cards

Topic levelA1— 5 findings, three of them about A1 words and two about A2 words. Read this when you next edit your deck.

noite and muito have no cards

You read noite on four cards — 0652 meia-noite, 0656 de noite, à noite, 0675 boa noite, and inside the second sentence of 0128 permanecer. You read muito on five: 0676, 0689, 0690, 0041 chove muito and 0151 Eu rio muito. Card 0665 is a sixth card, and gives a second form, muitas. No card teaches either word on its own. muito is one of the most used words in European Portuguese, and it changes form: muito, muita, muitos, muitas.

Suggested cards: noite (f, -s): night, muito (muita, -s): much, many, very

direito is only ever a direction

Cards 0571 and 0579 teach à direita and do lado direito. The word on its own also means right-hand, straight, and a legal right, and it is the everyday adjective.

Suggested card: direito (direita, -s): right, straight

Eleven of the eighteen cards have no sentence

The numbers (0597, 0607, 0617), the month (0643) and the seven phrases (0652, 0656, 0665, 0675, 0676, 0689, 0690) are bare: the front holds the English and the back holds the Portuguese again, with no example. All eleven had to be given a sentence written for this book, tagged new.

oito shows how odd that is. You read it in four real deck sentences on other cards — 0065 Eu durmo oito horas, 0118 O comboio parte às oito, 0154 Eu saio de casa às oito, 0172 Eu tenho um encontro às oito — and its own card 0597 still has none.

Suggested edit: give each one an example sentence. The ones on this page are a starting point.

Card 0361 receita reads oddly

The sentence is Eu gosto de receita. Portuguese wants an article or a plural here: Eu gosto da receita for a particular one, or Eu gosto de receitas in general. The page prints the card as it stands.

Suggested edit: Eu gosto desta receita. — I like this recipe.

Card 0712 exato is glossed as the wrong part of speech

The front says exactly. exato is an adjective and means exact. The adverb exactly is exatamente. The card also has no sentence, and no gender or plural forms, although exato takes them: exato, exata, exatos, exatas.

Suggested edit: exato (exata, -s): exact, with the sentence Este é o preço exato.

The colours mean something different on each layer. Here the colour is what the melted letter left in the word: blue = an i, orange = a u in the end, green = no letter at all, because the new i is not written today, purple = the word only looks as if the rule touched it. Grey marks anything no colour role produced: the contrast cards, the scope line for the missing words, and the deck note that covers more than one group. In the tables and the index each missing word is painted in its own result's colour. Colour is never the only channel — every result is also named in words.

About the level flags. A1 A2 B1 B2 C1 are CEFR levels. Your deck stores no level for nouns, adjectives or phrases. It does mark 145 verb cards (A1), but that is the level of the course book they were taken from, not a judgement about each word — it covers mergulhar “to dive” as readily as falar. So the flags here are still an estimate, and a few of them sit above that mark on purpose. They follow how common the word is in everyday European Portuguese and where it normally appears in A1–B1 course books. Treat them as a reading order, not as a rule. If a word feels easy and it says B1, learn it.

Where the origin is not firm. Two rows carry a dashed edge, and the two marks mean different things. deitado is marked starred form: the spoken-Latin verb behind it, *dēctāre, is worked back and was never written down. fato is marked sources disagree: many trace it to a Gothic word *fat, while the Portuguese dictionaries Priberam and Infopédia prefer an Arabic one, hatu. No source derives it from factum, which is the only claim this page needs.

Why oi and ou both exist. This is not settled in detail. What is agreed is that both have been in use for centuries, that Old Portuguese wrote some words both ways, and that different words later fixed on different spellings. The page says which word went which way and does not claim to explain why each one chose.
