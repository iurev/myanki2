Origin layer 01 · European Portuguese · 24 ch· cards · 16 words with no card · 10 cards for contrast
pl·cl·fl· → ch·

Latin words starting with pl-, cl- or fl- changed the same way, as long as people kept saying them.
In Portuguese they start with ch-.

The change hit every word people were still saying at the time. It worked inside a word too, when a prefix sat in front of the cluster. Spanish changed the same words but ended with ll-; Italian turned the l into i; French kept the cluster whole. So if you can see the Latin word, you can often guess the Portuguese one.

Sound law — a change in pronunciation that happened to every word with that sound, in that position. It works on all of them, not on a chosen few. If you know the law, you can predict words you have never seen.

Cluster — two consonants side by side with no vowel between them. pl, cl and fl are clusters. This page is about what Portuguese did with those three.

The law needed the cluster to begin a word, or to begin the part that comes after a prefix. af·flāre is ad plus flāre, and it became achar. In a few words the ch· was already made, and Portuguese only built it into a longer word later. Latin oculum also ends up with c beside l, once the u drops out. But there the pair sits between two vowels, and it gave lh instead: olho. That road is layer 04, not this page.

Where *plek- ended up in your deck

The old root *plek- meant “to fold”. Latin built the verb plicāre on it, and the ending ·plex “fold” as well. The six Portuguese words below all come from those forms. They came in by all three roads, and the deck teaches them as six separate cards.

Deck check

Six things this layer found in your own cards

Topic levelA1— 6 findings: four about A1 words, two about A2 words.

chorar and chuva have no cards A1

They are among the most common words the law made, and the deck has neither. You already meet chuva inside the levar card, in guarda-chuva, an umbrella. And the deck has chover, the verb, without chuva, the noun.

Suggested cards: chorar: to cry — A criança chora muito. / The child cries a lot., chuva (f, as chuvas): rain — Hoje há muita chuva. / Today there is a lot of rain.

fechar has no card, but fechado does A1

The deck teaches the adjective fechado, closed, and not the verb fechar, to close. fechar is A1 and you say it every day. The same gap shows up twice more on this page: chateado without chato, and preencher without encher.

Suggested cards: fechar: to close — Eu fecho a porta. / I close the door., chato (chata, -s): boring, annoying, encher: to fill

The bolacha card says I like cookie. A2

Two problems in one line. I like cookie is not English anybody says, and cookie is the American word: in Portugal a bolacha is a biscuit. The row on this page prints the corrected English, not the card's.

Suggested edit: Eu gosto de bolacha. / I like biscuits.

Two more English lines need one word each A2

The empregado card reads He is a employee. It needs an before a vowel sound. The explicação card reads Explanation is important. It needs The in front. Both rows on this page print the corrected English.

Suggested edits: He is an employee., The explanation is important.

The prato card is odd in both languages A1

It reads Eu gosto de prato. / I like dish. You like the food, not the plate, and I like dish is not a sentence. The chave and mochila cards already use a better frame for objects.

Suggested edit: Eu uso o prato. / I use the plate.

Three ch· cards carry no sentence A1

tchau, acho que sim and acho que não are bare phrase cards: the front holds the English, the back holds the Portuguese again. There is nothing to read aloud and nothing that shows how the phrase is used. The three rows in the word tables above carry a sentence written for this page, tagged new.

Suggested edit: give each back the two lines the noun cards have. For acho que não: Achas que ele vem? Acho que não. / Do you think he is coming? I don't think so.

About the level flags. A1 A2 B1 B2 C1 are CEFR levels. Your deck stores no level for nouns, adjectives or phrases. It does mark 145 verb cards (A1), but that is the level of the course book they were taken from, not a judgement about each word — it covers mergulhar “to dive” as readily as falar. Seven verb cards on this page carry that mark — chamar, chamar-se, chegar a, chover, cheirar a, preencher and explicar — and one of them, cheirar a, is flagged A2 here, one step above the deck. Layer 07 flags the same card the same way. An eighth verb, achar, carries a chapter marker instead, (ch.13), which is not a level. So the flags here are an estimate. They follow how common the word is in everyday European Portuguese and where it normally appears in A1–B1 course books. Treat them as a reading order, not as a rule. If a word feels easy and it says B1, learn it.

Where the origin is not firm. Four table rows carry a dashed Sources disagree mark, and each prints both readings; the third specimen at the top of the page carries the same mark. A word that comes back in a later table is marked once, on its first row. A plain grey row background is not a mark: it only says the row carries a note underneath. Dictionaries file cheirar a under flagrāre (to burn) or under frāgrāre (to smell sweet); the two were mixed up already in Latin. The meaning is the one of frāgrāre, but the sound settles the form: fr· stays fr· in Portuguese, so only fl· can have given this ch·. mochila came in from Spanish, but where Spanish found it is unsettled. fechado and fechar are both built on fecho, and fecho is most often traced to Vulgar Latin *pesclum, from pessulus “bolt”; Priberam calls that doubtful and Infopédia calls it obscure — but no reading sends the word back to pl·, cl· or fl·, which is why it sits with the words that only look the same. Three more things are firm but worth naming: the road from Greek platýs to chato runs through several steps; the centuries printed in the main chart are rough; and one word in the book group, flor, is not a late borrowing at all — it is already in the first Portuguese poems, and its row says so.
